LAX to SLC turbulence

Flights between Los Angeles (LAX) and Salt Lake City (SLC) fly about 590 miles, roughly 1½ hours in the air. In the past year, pilots near this route reported moderate bumps most often over California, and more often in March. Severe reports were rare: 18 of 1,765.

Where on the route it gets bumpy

Moderate bumps were reported most often over California, near Burbank, about 10 min after leaving LAX (1 h 15 min after leaving SLC): 146 of 180 reports there. Close to the ground, wind over buildings and land stirs the air. More on bumps near the ground.

The next most were near SLC, where flights climb out or come in to land, about 1 h 20 min after leaving LAX (5 min after leaving SLC): 109 of 307 reports there. Questions

Is LAX to SLC a bumpy flight?

Most flights see light chop at most. From November 2025 to October 2026, pilots near the LAX–SLC route filed 1,765 turbulence reports, and severe reports were rare: 18 of 1,765 (1.0%). Pilots report bumpy air more readily than smooth, so the share of flights with strong bumps is lower still. When is the smoothest time of year to fly LAX to SLC?

In the past year, moderate bumps made up a smaller share of reports in July and August and a bigger share in March. Any month can have a smooth or a bumpy day, so the forecast for your flight matters more.

Is SLC to LAX the same?

Yes. It's the same air, so bumps are reported in the same places, just at different times into the flight.

Are takeoffs and landings bumpy at LAX and SLC?

On windy days they can be, for a few minutes. LAX had gusts of 25 knots or more on 17 days in the past year, most often in February (5 days). SLC had gusts of 25 knots or more on 105 days in the past year, most often in July (14 days).

How we made this page

  • Period: November 2025 to October 2026. Pilot reports (PIREPs) and airport weather reports (METARs) from the US National Weather Service, through the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive at Iowa State University.
  • 1,765 pilot reports of turbulence from airliners within 60 nautical miles of the direct LAX–SLC path, near the height a flight would be there (BumpCast's flight model: a typical climb, cruise and descent). Real flights wander off the direct path, so this is the air near the route, not a record of every flight on it.
  • Airliners only: mainline and regional jets and regional turboprops, not light aircraft. Each report counts at the strongest turbulence it names; "light to moderate" is kept separate, and "moderate to severe" counts as severe.
  • Gusty days: days with at least one hourly airport report of gusts of 25 knots or more, the same threshold the app uses for a gusty takeoff or landing.
  • What the numbers can't say: pilots don't report on every flight, and habits differ: in the past year, smooth air made up 4% of cruise reports filed with Washington Center but 45% with Miami Center. So these pages show where and when bumps were reported, compared within a route. They aren't the odds for your flight, and we don't rank routes against each other.
