Forecast accuracy

How accurate is the turbulence outlook?

We checked BumpCast's global turbulence outlook against 441,678 reports from airline pilots over the US and Canada, a year of them. Where the outlook read bumpier, pilots really did report more bumps.

Checked against pilot reports

Useful, not perfect

Pick a pilot report of moderate bumps and a calmer one at random: the outlook rated the bumpier one higher about 7 times in 10. Forecasters call this the AUC, here 0.69, where 0.5 is a coin toss and 1 is perfect.

Higher readings, more bumps

Where the outlook read lowest (under 0.05 on the turbulence scale pilots and forecasters use), 5.7% of pilot reports at cruising height were moderate or stronger. Where it read 0.30 or more, 56% were. Severe bumps stayed rare: 2.1% of reports even at the highest readings.

What it means for your flight

A high outlook is worth heeding: keep your seat belt on and stow hot drinks. A low one isn't a promise of smooth air: even at the lowest readings, pilots reported at least light bumps 40% of the time, though pilots report bumpy air more readily than smooth. On its own, the outlook never shows more than light chop in the app. Only the US national turbulence forecast, an official warning or a recent pilot report can raise it, and this check shows why: even at the highest readings, more than 4 in 10 reports were lighter than moderate.

What we tested, and what we didn't

  • The global outlook. BumpCast's own clear-air turbulence estimate from two global weather models, ECMWF's and NOAA's GFS, averaged. The app uses it for international flights (Global outlooks, beta) and beyond the 18 hours the US National Weather Service's Graphical Turbulence Guidance (GTG) covers. GTG, the main forecast for flights in the US and southern Canada, isn't what's tested here.
  • The best case. We used each model's analysis, its picture of the weather at that hour, matched to every report within 3 hours. A forecast made a day or two ahead is less sharp.
  • Over North America. Pilots outside North America file too few reports in the archive we use to check the outlook there.
  • Year-long patterns are harder. Over a year, the outlook's calmer and bumpier months matched pilot reports on 7 of 16 US routes, and its bumpiest stretches on 2. That's why our international route pages show months only, as a rough guide.

Questions

How accurate are turbulence forecasts?

Useful, not perfect. We checked BumpCast's global outlook against 441,678 airline pilot reports over a year. Pick a report of moderate bumps and a calmer one at random, and the outlook rated the bumpier one higher about 7 times in 10. Where it read highest, 56% of reports were moderate or stronger; where it read lowest, 5.7%.

Does a smooth forecast mean no bumps at all?

No. Even where the outlook read lowest, pilots reported at least light bumps 40% of the time. Light chop is common and harmless, and pilots report bumpy air more readily than smooth. Keep your seat belt fastened while seated.

Can turbulence be predicted?

Much of it, yes. Clear-air turbulence and mountain waves come from the wind and temperature patterns weather models forecast, so higher model readings do go with more bumps. Bumps in and around thunderstorms and over small hills are harder, which is why pilots also steer around storms and share reports on the day.

How we made this page

  • Pilot reports: 441,678 turbulence reports from airliners (mainline and regional jets and regional turboprops) over the US and Canada, November 2025 to October 2026, from the US National Weather Service through the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive. Each counts at the strongest turbulence it names.
  • The outlook: 1,460 analyses, four a day, of ECMWF's open data and NOAA's GFS. BumpCast's turbulence estimate from each, uncapped, averaged over the two models and read at each report's place and height, from the analysis nearest its time. ECMWF alone scored 0.68 and GFS alone 0.69.
  • Contains ECMWF open data (CC BY 4.0). GFS data from NOAA.
